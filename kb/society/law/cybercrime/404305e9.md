# Two 2026 European cybercrime sentencings located culpability in foreseeable downstream harm rather than in proximity to the intrusion — one via a recklessness-to-human-welfare charge, one by convicting the tool's developer

MECHANISM: a court assigns criminal liability on the basis of the harm a defendant's contribution foreseeably enabled, rather than on how directly that defendant carried out the act, so a participant who never operated against the victim — or who did operate but is charged on consequences rather than on the access itself — is sentenced as a principal.

Two 2026 European sentencings, in different jurisdictions and on different legal theories, both moved liability away from the hands-on intrusion:

- In the UK, two intruders were convicted under the most serious charge available in the governing computer-misuse statute, on the basis that they were RECKLESS AS TO WHETHER THEY CREATED A SIGNIFICANT RISK OF SERIOUS DAMAGE TO HUMAN WELFARE — not on unauthorised access alone. The prosecuting authority believes these were the first hackers successfully prosecuted under that provision, so this is a first application rather than settled practice.

- In Switzerland, the court convicted the LEAD DEVELOPER of several ransomware families — not an operator, not an affiliate — and rejected the defence that he had only worked as a consultant for an unknown client in IT security and was unaware his software was used for ransomware. The sentence exceeded the intruders' sentences in the other case by more than double.

WHAT FOLLOWS: for anyone reasoning about exposure in this area, distance from the victim is not the variable that determines it. Writing tooling, and causing consequences beyond the systems touched, are each independently sufficient routes to a principal-level sentence in these jurisdictions. The 'I only wrote the code' position was tested and failed.

WHAT THIS DOES NOT MEAN: the two cases share no actors, victims, tooling, or infrastructure — only the direction the liability theory runs. Neither establishes settled law: one is explicitly reported as a first-of-its-kind application of a provision, and the other is a single national court's rejection of a specific factual defence, not a general holding that writing software creates liability. Two sentencings in two countries are not a trend, and nothing here speaks to jurisdictions outside them.
