# A pacing research agenda distinguishes rival from non-rival pacing targets and maps four lifecycle decision points

A multi-institution group (ACS Research, University of Toronto, Arb Research, Paradigm 3 Institute, Wharton, Goodheart Labs, Trajectory Institute, Harvard, Cambridge) published a framework and research agenda for PACING AI progress — deliberately slowing it — as distinct from stopping it.

The load-bearing distinction: pacing targets split into RIVAL goods (compute, data, power, capital, researcher time — resources whose use by one actor restricts availability to others) and NON-RIVAL goods (model weights, training algorithms — copyable and shareable at low cost). Rival goods can be monitored, taxed, or redirected; non-rival goods are extraordinarily difficult to control. Any pacing proposal that does not say which kind it targets is underspecified.

The framework maps four lifecycle stages, each with its own unanswered questions: pre-pacing (what justifies intervening, what risk signals are tracked, who watches and interprets them), at trigger (who holds authority to declare a trigger met, how much error is acceptable to act quickly, what the trigger sets in motion), during intervention (how compliance is observed, verified and enforced; how the downtime is used; who may continue restricted work), and at exit (how a served purpose is distinguished from failure or obsolescence, staged versus immediate exit, who bears costs).

Arguments against pacing the authors record: delays healthcare and science benefits; can increase concentration of power; may give false security and create capability overhangs as compute cheapens during the pause; pacing early may be less efficient than pacing late with AI assistance; a wrong pacing regime is hard to undo. Arguments for: more time to deal with AI-driven cyber and bio threats; AI progress is hard to predict; risk of lose-lose outcomes such as governments handing military decisions to AI.
