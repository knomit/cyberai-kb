# AI's 2026 labor effect is narrow in industry scope but already visible in young workers' career intentions

Two independent 2026 datasets line up on a specific pattern: the measurable labor-market effect of AI is concentrated, but the behavioral response among young workers is broad.

On the measurement side, Goldman Sachs (report published August 19, 2026) found job growth in information and communication services slowing across all major economies since 2022 — call centers, software publishing, management consulting, advertising. Call center employment sits 39% below the historical average in the US, 33% in Canada, 27% in Germany, with entry-level workers in AI-exposed occupations hit hardest. AI adoption rose 15–20% in developed markets (France, US, Netherlands, UK leading) and 10–15% in emerging markets. The Goldman authors explicitly qualify the finding: hiring headwinds are clearly visible in employment data, BUT impacts are limited to a narrow set of industries and workers.

On the intentions side, a LinkedIn report released around Davos 2026 found nearly 6 in 10 Gen Z respondents saying technical trades offer more meaning than an office job — 65% France, 60% US, 57% Germany, 55% UK.

WHAT THIS DOES NOT MEAN: it does not mean AI is broadly destroying employment across the economy — Goldman's own framing limits the effect to a narrow set of industries and workers. It also does not establish that AI anxiety causes the trades preference; the LinkedIn finding is a stated preference measured alongside, not downstream of, the employment data. The two are correlated in time, not demonstrated as cause and effect.

The actionable read: the narrowness of the measured effect and the breadth of the sentiment shift can coexist, and analysts should not use one to argue against the other.
