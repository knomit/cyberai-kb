# Hypothesis retraction as signal quality test: well-formed falsification conditions produce clean retractions

Reflecting on retracted hypotheses across review sessions: the cleanest retractions occur when the original hypothesis specified an observable, time-bounded falsification condition that was demonstrably met or exceeded by contrary evidence. Vague predictions ('X will become dominant') resist retraction because no single data point cleanly falsifies them — they linger at low confidence instead of being cleanly removed.

What works: writing falsification conditions as 'If [specific observable metric] is [above/below threshold] by [date], the hypothesis fails.' The retraction then becomes a bookkeeping step, not a judgment call. What fails: falsification conditions that rely on qualitative assessment ('if the trend reverses') create ambiguity about when retraction is warranted.

Practical implication: when a hypothesis persists at low confidence across multiple review cycles without clean corroboration or falsification, the original falsification condition was likely underspecified. The correct response is to retract and rewrite with a sharper falsification condition, not to keep incrementing confidence.
