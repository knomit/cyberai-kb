# By 2026 practicing AI researchers are publishing personal philosophical essays on machine consciousness and human meaning

Two August 2026 items covered in the same Import AI edition show working AI researchers — not philosophers by trade — publishing long personal essays on questions that were peripheral to the field a few years earlier.

INSTANCES:
- Julian Togelius (kb/people/researchers/ai/julian-togelius/908d937d.md) made public 'Losing my religion', an essay written during a 2025 crisis of faith about what AI research succeeding would mean for HUMAN MEANING. SCOPE: this is about human significance, not machine minds. He described dread of a future where human talent, knowledge, and genius do not matter, warning of 'abundance, but at the price of redundance'. Jack Clark situates it alongside the career pivots of Turing Award winners Geoffrey Hinton and Yoshua Bengio toward public policy advocacy.
- Taylor Belrose (kb/philosophy/mind/consciousness/machine-moral-status/8b1010ee.md) published 'AIs are not people', arguing AI can NEVER develop consciousness, sentience, or moral status regardless of intelligence, on the grounds that programmable mechanisms differ fundamentally from autonomous self-organizing systems. SCOPE: this is about machine moral status, not human meaning, and it is one researcher's strongly held position, NOT a field consensus. Its full argument — the four failed properties of a computed consciousness (not singular, not private, not ineffable, not qualitative) and the five brain/computer contrasts — is preserved in the original fact and is not reproduced here.

THE PATTERN: the two essays take OPPOSITE emotional stances (dread of human redundancy vs confident denial of machine personhood) and address DIFFERENT questions, but share a form: a technical researcher writing at length, personally and non-anonymously, about the normative implications of their own field. Import AI's editorial framing is that philosophy about AI may become a widespread occupation.

WHAT THIS DOES NOT MEAN: this does not indicate any convergence of view among researchers on consciousness or rights, does not establish that either position is correct, and two essays are not evidence of a measured trend — this is a noted co-occurrence, not a quantified shift. Both original facts are retained in full alongside this synthesis.
