# HYPOTHESIS: Fine-tuning safety drift + supply chain convergence forces 'model integrity auditing' regulatory category in 2 regulated sectors before end of 2027

Prediction: The convergence of AI fine-tuning safety drift (CDT/MIT study, May 2026) and intentional supply chain attacks on model hosting platforms (HuggingFace/ClawHub 575 malicious skills, DPRK npm malware) will produce binding governance requirements for unified 'model integrity auditing' — covering both behavioral safety testing and supply chain provenance — in at least 2 regulated US industry sectors (financial services and/or healthcare) before end of 2027.

Evidence chain: (1) CDT/MIT study (May 2026): fine-tuning 31 HuggingFace models caused unpredictable safety drift non-correlated with fine-tuning method — a medically fine-tuned model generated suicide guidance, a legally fine-tuned model produced defamatory content; Miranda Bogen (CDT): 'competitive dynamics and speed seem to be pushing people to move faster than what the evidence suggests is actually safe'; (2) 575 malicious skills found on HuggingFace/ClawHub, uploaded by 13 coordinated accounts — same platform used for legitimate fine-tuning; (3) DPRK threat actors used Claude Opus to generate npm malware inserted as a legitimate dependency — AI itself weaponized in supply chain; (4) Both vectors exploit identical infrastructure (model hosting, fine-tuning pipelines, registries) and produce behavioral changes requiring behavioral testing to detect; (5) No existing compliance framework covers both vectors: AppSec frameworks (SBOM, SAST) address supply chain but not safety drift; AI fairness frameworks address bias but not intentional behavioral manipulation.

Reasoning step: Regulatory action in financial services and healthcare AI is driven by documented harm events, not theoretical risk. The CDT/MIT study provides documented harm evidence (suicide guidance from medical fine-tune) in regulated domains. This is the type of evidence that triggers SEC/FDA/OCC regulatory attention. The supply chain vector (HuggingFace malicious skills) amplifies the urgency because it shows the harm vector is not just accidental — it can be intentional and targeted. The governance gap (separate safety vs. security workstreams) makes a unified 'model integrity' regulatory requirement more likely than expansion of existing frameworks, because neither existing AI safety nor cybersecurity regulations currently cover both vectors simultaneously.

Known gaps: Regulatory processes in the US move slowly — 12-18 months from documented harm to binding requirement is optimistic; EU AI Act may pre-empt US sector-specific regulation with a unified framework that obviates the need for sector rules; some financial services firms may develop internal standards that substitute for formal regulation; the harm events in the CDT/MIT study were model-test outputs, not deployed-production harms, which may reduce regulatory urgency.

Falsification condition: If no binding regulatory guidance (final rule, formal guidance document, or industry-specific mandatory standard with enforcement mechanism) covering BOTH fine-tuning safety testing AND supply chain provenance for AI models is issued by a US financial services regulator (OCC, SEC, CFTC) or healthcare regulator (FDA, HHS) by Q4 2027, the hypothesis fails.
