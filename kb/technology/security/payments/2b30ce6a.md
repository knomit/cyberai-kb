# Unsigned, unbound duplicate fields are an exploitable seam in EMV contactless protocols

The Zombie Card work illustrates a general protocol-design failure mode: when the same semantic value is carried in two places consumed by two different parties, and neither the binding between them nor at least one of the copies is cryptographically protected, an in-path relay can desynchronize them. In Visa's Kernel 3, card expiry appears as the Application Expiration Date in TLV tag 5F24 (evaluated by the terminal) and inside Track 2 Equivalent Data in tag 57 (evaluated by the issuer); Kernel 3 does not require the two to be consistently bound and the fDDA signature the terminal verifies excludes 5F24, so rewriting 5F24 to any date later than the transaction date leaves both the card signature and the issuer cryptogram valid. Kernel 3 additionally forwards all-zero Terminal Verification Results, removing the issuer's ability to see whether the terminal's local expiry check ran or failed — a second instance of the same seam, where the party with authority to decline lacks the evidence to do so.

The defense that worked in practice is a consistency check, not stronger cryptography: Mastercard's Kernel 2 compares the two expiry representations during READ RECORD parsing and treats a mismatch as a card data error, declining rather than falling back online. This is the actionable pattern — where a protocol carries redundant copies of a value, either bind them under a signature or have the verifying party cross-check them.

Scope and caveats that must travel with this synthesis: the attack requires physical possession of, or sustained NFC proximity to, the expired card; a man-in-the-middle relay between card and terminal; the account remaining open under the same primary account number (standard practice when an issuer sends a replacement card); and the issuing bank not independently re-checking expiry during authorization. It breaks no cryptography. Kernel-level and bank-level behavior varied in testing: across three banks tested with expired and replaced physical cards, one allowed the modification and accepted transactions from more than one card at once, one detected the modification and accepted transactions from only one active card, and one running Discover's kernel detected the modification but still accepted transactions from more than one card — so detection at the kernel does not by itself imply a decline at the issuer. Presented at the 35th USENIX Security Symposium, Baltimore, August 12–14, 2026; as of August 20, 2026 no advisory or mitigation guidance had been published by Visa, EMVCo, Mastercard, Discover, American Express or terminal vendor SumUp.
