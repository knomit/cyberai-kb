# ShinyHunters is credited with four distinct 2026 intrusion methods, from zero-day RCE to no-vulnerability access abuse, so a profile built from any one prescribes the wrong defence

Four 2026 records attribute activity to the same extortion crew by methods that demand disjoint defences. A defender who has read only one of them will size the wrong programme.

TRACK A — EXPLOITATION OF AN UNPATCHED FLAW. ShinyHunters, tracked by Google Mandiant as UNC6240, exploited CVE-2026-35273 in Oracle PeopleSoft Enterprise PeopleTools (CVSS 9.8, unauthenticated RCE) between 27 May and 9 June 2026. Oracle did not publish its advisory until 10 June, so it was a zero-day for the whole campaign. The bug needs no login and no user interaction, only HTTP network access, and exposure comes from an internet-reachable Environment Management Hub. Universities were hit hardest.

TRACK B — NO FLAW EXPLOITED AT ALL. Microsoft mapped three Salesforce attack paths tied to a year of ShinyHunters activity in which attackers 'walked into corporate Salesforce environments without exploiting any flaw in the platform itself, relying instead on social engineering and abuse of legitimate access.'

TRACK C — THIRD-PARTY SUPPLY-CHAIN ESCALATION INTO A CLOUD IDENTITY. Vercel disclosed a breach originating from the compromise of Context.ai, a third-party AI tool used by a Vercel employee; the attacker used that access to take over the employee's Google Workspace account and reach Vercel environments and non-sensitive environment variables. A Context.ai employee had been compromised with Lumma Stealer in February 2026, and a Context.ai AWS incident in March 2026 exposed OAuth tokens. The chain runs third-party AI tool → employee cloud account → internal environment. ShinyHunters claimed responsibility.

TRACK D — AUTOMATED MASS RECONNAISSANCE. Reco's research records ShinyHunters scanning thousands of Salesforce Sites with a modified 'AuraInspector', and notes that such groups are increasingly folding LLMs into reconnaissance and exploitation workflows.

WHY THIS MATTERS: the four tracks are answered by four different programmes. Track A by inventorying internet-exposed enterprise back-office endpoints and reducing their exposure — patching speed alone does not answer a zero-day. Track B by connected-app inventory, grant scope reduction, revocation speed, and social-engineering resistance. Track C by third-party/vendor risk management over the small tools individual employees adopt, and by limiting what an employee's cloud identity reaches. Track D by assuming discovery is exhaustive and cheap, so 'obscure enough not to be found' is not a control. No single one of these covers another.

SCOPE AND CAVEATS — ATTRIBUTION IS THE WEAK LINK ON EVERY TRACK. Track B is attributed to 'ShinyHunters (AND LOOKALIKES)'; Microsoft's mapping does not isolate this crew. Track C rests on the actor's OWN CLAIM of responsibility, which is not independent verification. Track D comes from a vendor (Reco) research piece and that record carries sources: 0 in this corpus, as do the Track A and Track B records. Track A is a dated ~two-week campaign while Track B spans a year, so the periods overlap without being co-extensive. WHAT THIS DOES NOT MEAN: this does NOT establish that one team ran all four, that the tracks were coordinated, or that the actor has abandoned any of them. It does not claim method diversity is unusual for extortion crews. It claims only that this corpus attributes all four to this name, and that a defence sized from any one is blind to the others. Treat the count of four as a floor set by what has been reported and recorded here, not as a census of the actor's methods.
