# Two 2026 persistence techniques survive the standard cleanup step because the foothold lives outside what that step resets

MECHANISM: the routine remediation resets one component, but the attacker's foothold lives in a different component that the remediation does not touch, so the compromise survives the cleanup and re-establishes itself.

INSTANCES.
- A WordPress backdoor (Sucuri) stores its payload in at least eight places across files, the database and shared memory, each able to rebuild the others; deleting a plugin, drop-in or theme copy is not enough. Remediation must remove ALL persistence locations (including database and shared-memory copies) together, AND the entry point.
- A rogue external MFA provider (Varonis 'TrustSink') placed in a legitimate sign-in flow captures plaintext passwords while returning a valid signed token; resetting a captured password does not remove the rogue provider, which then captures the replacement. This requires an attacker who ALREADY holds high privileges in the identity tenant to register the provider.

CONSEQUENCE: after a compromise, verify that the cleanup step actually removes the persistence mechanism, not just its visible effect — a password reset or single-file deletion can look complete while the foothold remains.

WHAT THIS DOES NOT MEAN: this does not claim password resets or file deletion are useless, nor that these two techniques are related or share an actor. It is two instances, both vendor-reported research, not a measured frequency. The TrustSink case is post-compromise persistence, not an initial-access technique.
