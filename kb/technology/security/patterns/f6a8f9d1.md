# Mid-2026 attacker tradecraft consistently optimizes for cost, repeatability and attribution-resistance rather than novelty

MERGED from two records of the same economics thesis: one a four-case survey of cost- and attribution-optimising infrastructure choices, one a shorter principle about repeatable playbooks and per-attempt cost. Both evidence sets and both caveats are preserved.

THE THESIS. Across several unrelated mid-2026 threat reports, the recurring design goal is NOT a cleverer exploit but a CHEAPER, HARDER-TO-TRACE, MORE REPEATABLE operation. Threat actors optimise for repeatability and per-attempt cost rather than sophistication.

## THE FOUR INFRASTRUCTURE CASES
- FREE INFRASTRUCTURE: EtherHiding operators shifted C2 to the BNB Smart Chain testnet, where writes are free, unlimited and leave NO FINANCIAL TRAIL — Israel's National Digital Agency notes malware C2 backends now run entirely on a testnet. EtherHiding simultaneously serves as delivery backend, C2 channel, victim database and skimming infrastructure.
- REUSED CODE, EXTENDED REACH: Evooo1Bot (Fortinet FortiGuard Labs, active since July 2026) reuses the leaked Mirai DDoS engine and adds encrypted C2, an SSH brute-force scanner, a SOCKS relay module, a credential sniffer and an exploit arsenal for OLD KNOWN FLAWS (including CVE-2007-3010 and CVE-2016-6277) — converting edge devices into proxy infrastructure rather than building anything new.
- CREDENTIALS OVER ZERO-DAYS: the 'TheHatman' Azure/Entra exfiltration campaign leaked employee databases from McDonald's, TCS, Vodafone, HCL Technologies, Kyndryl, Gap, Hexaware and Wyndham Hotels; Hudson Rock ASSESSES it most likely originates from targeted exploitation of infostealer infections rather than a systemic Azure zero-day.
- TRANSITIVE REACH: 95% of organisations hit by the LiteLLM supply chain attack (attributed to TeamPCP) were ALREADY exposed through the earlier Trivy scanner compromise, and did not need to install LiteLLM directly — it arrived through DSPy, MLflow, CrewAI, OpenHands and Arize Phoenix, executing at Python startup without a LiteLLM import.

## THE REPEATABLE-PLAYBOOK CASE, CARRIED FROM THE MERGED RECORD
THN's analysis argues the same thing from the social-engineering side: THE MOST COMMON INITIAL ACCESS LAST YEAR WAS SIMPLY TO ASK THE VICTIM. The representative pattern is the fake 'prove you are not a robot' page that silently places a command on the visitor's clipboard and then talks them into running it — a playbook that costs NEARLY NOTHING PER ATTEMPT and can therefore be run at enormous volume.

## DEFENSIVE IMPLICATION
Prioritisation models that rank threats by EXPLOIT SOPHISTICATION will systematically underweight this class. The cheap paths — stolen credentials, unpatched decade-old edge-device CVEs, transitive dependencies, free blockchain infrastructure, and user-executed clipboard commands — carry the volume.

## WHAT THIS DOES NOT MEAN
- This is a pattern observed across contemporaneous reports, NOT A MEASURED SHIFT in attacker behaviour over time, and it does NOT imply novel exploitation has declined — the same period saw genuine zero-day work (Lazarus on CVE-2026-68820) and original baseband research (the Unisoc VoLTE chain).
- It does NOT mean the attribution assessments here are settled: the Hudson Rock initial-vector claim is EXPLICITLY AN ASSESSMENT, and the LightSpy Chinese-contractor attribution rests partly on an operator's operational-security error.
- The 'most common initial access last year was to ask the victim' claim is a vendor-newsletter analysis, not a measured share, and both input records were single-sourced.
