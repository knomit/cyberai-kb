# Mid-2026 attacker tradecraft consistently optimizes for cost and attribution-resistance rather than novelty

Across several unrelated mid-2026 threat reports, the recurring design goal is not a cleverer exploit but a cheaper, harder-to-trace operation. The pattern shows up in four distinct places:

- Free infrastructure: EtherHiding operators shifted C2 to the BNB Smart Chain testnet, where writes are free, unlimited and leave no financial trail — Israel's National Digital Agency notes malware C2 backends now run entirely on a testnet. EtherHiding simultaneously serves as delivery backend, C2 channel, victim database and skimming infrastructure.
- Reused code, extended reach: Evooo1Bot (Fortinet FortiGuard Labs, active since July 2026) reuses the leaked Mirai DDoS engine and adds encrypted C2, an SSH brute-force scanner, a SOCKS relay module, a credential sniffer and an exploit arsenal for old known flaws (including CVE-2007-3010 and CVE-2016-6277) — converting edge devices into proxy infrastructure rather than building anything new.
- Credentials over zero-days: the 'TheHatman' Azure/Entra exfiltration campaign leaked employee databases from McDonald's, TCS, Vodafone, HCL Technologies, Kyndryl, Gap, Hexaware and Wyndham Hotels; Hudson Rock assesses it most likely originates from targeted exploitation of infostealer infections rather than a systemic Azure zero-day.
- Transitive reach: 95% of organizations hit by the LiteLLM supply chain attack (attributed to TeamPCP) were already exposed through the earlier Trivy scanner compromise, and did not need to install LiteLLM directly — it arrived through DSPy, MLflow, CrewAI, OpenHands and Arize Phoenix, executing at Python startup without a LiteLLM import.

Defensive implication: prioritization models that rank threats by exploit sophistication will systematically underweight this class. The cheap paths — stolen credentials, unpatched decade-old edge-device CVEs, transitive dependencies, and free blockchain infrastructure — carry the volume.

WHAT THIS DOES NOT MEAN: this is a pattern observed across four contemporaneous reports, not a measured shift in attacker behavior over time, and it does not imply novel exploitation has declined — the same period saw genuine zero-day work (Lazarus on CVE-2026-68820) and original baseband research (the Unisoc VoLTE chain). It also does not mean attribution assessments here are settled: the Hudson Rock initial-vector claim is explicitly an assessment, and the LightSpy Chinese-contractor attribution rests partly on an operator's operational-security error.
