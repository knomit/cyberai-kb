# Domain reputation is transferable, making age-based trust heuristics a purchasable commodity

Two Infoblox findings combine into a structural weakness in reputation-based defenses: dropcatch re-registration is high-volume and routine (roughly 50,400/day in gTLDs alone in H1 2026, ~65,000/day including ccTLDs, i.e. nearly 20% — one in five — of all daily gTLD and ccTLD registrations, led at TLD level by .net and .xyz ahead of .com, and brokered largely through GoDaddy, Namecheap and DropCatch.com), and at least one threat actor (Sable Squirrel) has spent close to $7 million exploiting exactly that channel to build an enterprise spanning illegal sports streaming, gambling promotion and malware infrastructure. The pattern: because a re-registered domain inherits aged registration history, backlinks, residual traffic and reputation signals, any defense that treats domain age or accumulated reputation as evidence of trustworthiness is treating a purchasable attribute as an intrinsic one. The inherited assets are not limited to reputation scores — they include email intended for the original holder, cached search results, inherited web traffic, lingering DNS records, and sometimes a ready-made code-injection foothold on already-compromised sites. SCOPE: the ~20% figure covers H1 2026 gTLD+ccTLD registrations and describes dropcatch registrations overall, not malicious ones; most dropcatching is done by legitimate domain investors and by defenders precautionarily catching expired domains. WHAT THIS DOES NOT MEAN: it does not mean one in five new domains is malicious, nor that domain age is worthless as a signal — only that it is not self-authenticating and should not be weighted as though it could not be bought.
