# The one takedown this corpus follows up on did not hold, and it is the only one it follows up on at all

This corpus records a large number of law-enforcement disruptions of criminal platforms and infrastructure. For exactly one of them does it also record what happened afterwards, and the answer was: the activity continued.

THE TAKEDOWN. INTERPOL's Operation Ramz (October 2025 – February 2026, announced June 2026) disrupted Sniper Dz, a phishing-as-a-service platform active since at least 2015. By any structural measure it was thorough: 201 arrests across 13 MENA countries; 'Guedz', the primary developer and administrator, arrested by the Algerian National Police; the website and hardware holding phishing scripts seized; more than 45,000 victim records recovered.

THE AFTERMATH. In the same month's reporting, Group-IB tied live campaigns against MENA users — fake Facebook accounts impersonating politicians, public figures and trusted organisations, promoting fake free mobile internet, financial compensation and government subsidy offers, redirecting through intermediary sites to phishing and traffic-monetisation infrastructure — to Sniper Dz, which it described as taken down in the INTERPOL-led operation but appearing TO PERSIST.

WHY THIS IS NOT SURPRISING FOR THIS PARTICULAR PLATFORM: the takedown record itself notes Sniper Dz had PREVIOUSLY REBRANDED as Joker Dz, Storm Dz and Spam Dz. A platform with a demonstrated history of surviving under new names is the wrong one to treat as decapitated by seizing the name it currently uses.

WHAT IS ACTUALLY LOAD-BEARING HERE IS THE ABSENCE. A query of this corpus for takedown records returns many — Amadey/StealC, SocGholish under Operation Endgame, AudiA6, NetNut, Stark Industries, 94 Ukrainian call centres, the Kratos phishing kit, and others — each recorded at the moment of announcement, with arrest counts, seizure counts and recovered-record counts. As of this writing NONE of the others carries any subsequent record of whether the disruption held. The single case where the corpus happens to contain a later observation is also the single case where the disruption is reported not to have held. That is one observation, not a rate — but it means the corpus's apparent evidence that takedowns work consists entirely of announcements, and announcements measure the operation rather than its effect.

SCOPE AND CAVEATS: the persistence claim is Group-IB's characterisation ('appears to persist'), single-sourced, and not a measured continuation rate. The timing is imprecise in the sources: the operation ran October 2025 – February 2026 while both records date to June 2026 reporting, and the campaign record's 'the prior month' most plausibly refers to the takedown's ANNOUNCEMENT rather than the operation itself — so how long after the disruption the activity resumed is NOT established here. Both source records carry sources: 0 in this corpus.

WHAT THIS DOES NOT MEAN: this does NOT claim takedowns are ineffective, and it does not diminish the outcomes actually achieved — 201 arrests and 45,000 recovered victim records are real regardless of whether the platform resumed. It does NOT generalise from one case to a base rate, and it does not claim the other takedowns listed above failed; the point is precisely that the corpus does not say either way. It also does not claim the persisting campaigns are run by the same operators — an arrested developer's platform can be continued by customers or imitators, and the sources do not distinguish these.

Falsification: a follow-up record showing Sniper Dz-attributed activity ceasing after Operation Ramz would overturn the specific case; follow-up records on the other listed takedowns would remove the absence this fact is mostly about.
